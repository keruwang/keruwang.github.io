Cognition-in-Motion: Agents That Infer Latent Goals as Intent Takes Shape

A proactive desktop agent, and a human-centered benchmark for evaluating it

Keru Wang, with Janet Johnson; mentored by Saining Xie

Ongoing

About

People rarely sit down at a computer with a finished goal. As they search, read and compare, what they find in the task reshapes what they want, so the real goal often goes unstated and keeps moving. We are building a proactive desktop agent that watches natural computer use, reasons about the latent goal behind it and how the task is shaping it, and offers the help that brings the person closer to that goal.

Concretely, the agent reads the screen as the person works, keeps track of which earlier context still matters, and predicts the next step toward a goal the person has not stated. The aim is not to take over the task but to make that next step easier, for example by bringing back something seen earlier, opening what the person is about to need, or drafting what comes next, and to step in only when it helps.

Drawing on my background in HCI, I am making the evaluation human-centered. In real life there is no single ground truth for what someone intends, or for which help is the right one: the goal is still forming, and different people, or the same person on another day, would welcome different next steps. So instead of scoring agents against one scripted goal, the benchmark treats both the goal and good help as a distribution. Its tasks come from real scenarios, from stated to unstated goals and from step-by-step to open-ended work, and an agent's help is judged against what people actually go on to do and what they endorse in hindsight.

ReTrace: An Ambient, Personal Room-Based Agent That Observes, Understands, and Supports

Learns your routines, tasks and goals from how you interact with the objects in your room

Keru Wang, Reina Mun

Ongoing

About

ReTrace is an ambient, personal multimodal AI agent based in a person's own room. It observes how that person interacts with the physical objects around them, such as the kettle they come back to, the plant they water or the half-finished project on the desk, and learns from those interactions what their routines, ongoing tasks and goals are. It does this in a personal way, since the same object can mean something different for different people. To follow a person over weeks rather than moments, it keeps an object-oriented, long-horizon memory in which each object holds a history of how it was used and what that use meant.

Rather than speaking through a phone or a chat window, ReTrace supports the person through ambient cues from the room itself, such as light, or a projection onto a surface or the object in question, to help them pick a task back up, remember something at the right moment or keep a habit going. We focus the evaluation on well-being: whether this support makes daily life in the room better for the person, not only whether the agent's guesses are right.

SEAM: Situated Executable Associative Memory for a Co-located Community

A shared memory for a community that connects, reveals, and acts

Keru Wang, Michael Xu, Ken Perlin, Nebojsa Jojic, Andrzej Banburski-Fahey

Preprint on arXiv

arXiv | PDF

Abstract

In a group, what each person knows sits apart from everyone else's, and whether those pieces ever meet depends on who happens to talk to whom. Taking inspiration from how a single mind works with memory, we built a shared memory that does the same for a community, prototyped as a social app. Members post everyday moments; LLM agents work out how fragments relate, distill knowledge about the group, and reply in a situated and associative way, while instructions embedded in posts shape how the system behaves. We deployed SEAM to 46 people in a co-located community with no task assigned and ran a follow-up workshop with 14. Members shared their days, questioned the memory about their community, and instructed it to track routines and run games, and its replies introduced them to people and subjects they had never encountered.

An Investigation of Proactive AI Agents for Reducing Idea Stagnation in Group Brainstorming

A proactive agent that steps in when group ideation stalls

Keru Wang, Pincun Liu, Saining Xie, Ken Perlin

Under review — preprint available upon request

Abstract

We designed a proactive AI agent that monitors group brainstorming and intervenes at opportune moments to counter idea stagnation. Through two user studies with 18 participants, we found that the proactive agent boosted idea generation, depth, and novelty compared to a reactive baseline, though users still preferred reactive support for its greater sense of control.

Should I Speak Up? Aligning Proactive AI Interventions with Human Expectations in Group Brainstorming

When should an AI speak up in a group discussion?

Keru Wang, Pincun Liu, Saining Xie, Ken Perlin

Under review — preprint available upon request

Abstract

We explore how proactive AI agents can step in at the right moments to keep group brainstorming productive and natural. Using a custom LLM system that monitors discussions and explains its reasoning, we compared its timing judgement to expert evaluations. The study uncovers what makes AI interventions feel helpful versus disruptive, guiding the design of more intuitive and collaborative AI partners.

Flying Together: Human-Guided Immersive Shared Control for Aerial Robot Teams in Unknown Environments

VR shared control for guiding teams of drones

Lou De Bel-Air, Luca Morando, Ruitao Chen, Keru Wang, Benjamin Jarvis, Charbel Toumieh, Yang Zhou, Ken Perlin, Dario Floreano, Giuseppe Loianno

IEEE ICRA 2026

arXiv | PDF | Video

Abstract

We developed a VR-based shared control framework that allows operators to guide teams of drones in complex, unknown environments. A motion-primitive planner integrates real-time user input to generate collision-free trajectories, while an admittance controller enables flexible human influence. Experiments show that this immersive, human-in-the-loop approach enhances obstacle avoidance, coordination, and operator efficiency.

Celestial Blossom: A Collaborative, Meditative VR Journey of Fractal Art and Music

Co-creating fractal art and music together in VR

Pincun Liu, Keru Wang, Yi Wu, Xiaoan Liu, Yushen Hu, Zhu Wang, Agnieszka Roginska, Ken Perlin

ACM SIGGRAPH Asia XR 2025

ACM | PDF | Video

Abstract

Celestial Blossom is a collaborative, meditative VR experience where participants co-create generative fractal art through hand-drawn sketches, transforming movements into evolving audiovisual symphonies. Set in a celestial void, strokes morph into glowing fractal particles, from microscopic grains to cosmic stars, each shaping sonic textures and evolving into an immersive soundscape. Multiplayer interaction allows users to weave their creations into a shared immersive composition. At the end, participants receive a printed rendering of their artwork, which can be scanned to revisit the fractals and melodies they created in VR.

SandScape

A sand table for shaping landscapes by hand, with terrain analysis projected back onto the sand

Keru Wang (lead artist), with the MIT Tangible Media Group

Permanent exhibition, MIT Museum, 2022

About | Live simulator | Gallery

About

A table of sand for shaping landscapes by hand. As the sand moves, the table reads its new shape and projects an analysis of the terrain back onto it: its height, its contour lines, and where rain would run off it, while a screen beside it shows the same landscape in 3D. I led the design and development of this version, made with the MIT Tangible Media Group; it is on permanent view in the MIT Museum's Radical Atoms exhibition. The first SandScape was made in 2003 by Hiroshi Ishii's Tangible Media Group with the Senseable City Lab.
